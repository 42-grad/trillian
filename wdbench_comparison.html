Trillian vs. the field

On the 930 basic-graph-pattern queries — the ones where Trillian returns the exact same result counts as every published engine — its median answer is 1 ms (single) and 132 ms (multiple): roughly 9× to 70× under the fastest of Blazegraph, Jena, Virtuoso and Neo4j. On property paths and C2RPQs it’s fast but currently under-produces — shown honestly below.

Read this together with the latency chart. A 3 ms median on paths only covers the 462 of 660 queries that completed; the other 198 timed out or hit the cap. The published engines likewise carry heavy timeouts here (Neo4j 134, Jena 96 on paths; Jena 242 on C2RPQ).

BGP is provably correct — 930 queries, zero deviations — so the latency win on those classes is real. Reading the comparison fairly

  • Different hardware & setup. The published numbers are from the WDBench paper’s machine; Trillian ran on an AWS r6i.16xlarge. Absolute milliseconds are indicative, not a controlled head-to-head.
  • In-memory vs. disk-backed. Much of the latency gap is architectural — Trillian is a read-only in-RAM store with flat-CSR indexes and u32 IDs; the others are general-purpose DBMSs paging from disk with full transactional machinery.
  • Our error = their timeout. Where Trillian shows “error” it’s the row-cap firing on operators we haven’t pipelined yet; a comparable engine would typically time out instead. Both mean “did not complete.”
  • Same queries, verified. Identical WDBench query sets (280/681/498/660/539), 60 s timeout, 100 k output cap — and BGP result counts checked one-by-one against the published engines.
  • Single run. One measured pass per query (warm), no averaging across runs.
